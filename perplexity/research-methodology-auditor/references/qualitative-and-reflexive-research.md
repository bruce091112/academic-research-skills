# Qualitative & Reflexive Research Core — Perplexity Edition

This reference provides platform-neutral qualitative-research safeguards for research design, data collection, coding, interpretation, triangulation, and researcher reflexivity.

## 1. Methodological coherence

Use qualitative methods when the research question seeks meaning, experience, process, context, interpretation, culture, practice, or theory generation.

Align:
- research question;
- paradigm / epistemological stance;
- sampling logic;
- data-generation method;
- analytic approach;
- quality criteria;
- reporting language.

Do not use qualitative terminology decoratively. If thematic analysis, grounded theory, phenomenology, narrative inquiry, ethnography, or case study is claimed, the actual procedure should match the named approach.

## 2. Sampling

State the sampling logic explicitly:
- purposive;
- criterion;
- maximum variation;
- theoretical;
- snowball;
- convenience, with limitations;
- complete / bounded-case sampling.

Do not justify sample adequacy by a universal numeric threshold. Discuss informational adequacy, variation, depth, analytic purpose, and stopping logic.

If saturation is claimed, specify what kind of saturation is meant and how it was assessed. Do not use saturation as a generic synonym for 'we stopped interviewing.'

## 3. Data generation

For interviews, observations, documents, artifacts, diaries, or field notes:
- distinguish planned prompts from emergent follow-up;
- record when and why probes changed;
- avoid leading questions that embed the desired answer;
- preserve participant language where analytically important;
- separate observation from interpretation in field notes where feasible;
- distinguish researcher-generated documents from participant-generated data.

Examples supplied to participants may scaffold understanding but can also prime responses. When examples are used, document them and consider their influence during reflexive analysis.

## 4. Coding and analysis

Qualitative coding should preserve a traceable path from source material to interpretation.

A useful minimum audit trail is:

`raw excerpt / observation -> initial code -> category -> theme / analytic concept -> interpretation -> relation to RQ`

Rules:
- do not invent codes without data support;
- retain negative cases and disconfirming evidence;
- distinguish descriptive coding from interpretive coding;
- document code merges, splits, renaming, and definition changes;
- keep code definitions stable enough for consistent use, while allowing explicit iteration;
- do not treat frequency alone as thematic importance.

## 5. Theme development

A theme should represent a patterned meaning relevant to the RQ, not merely a topic label.

For each theme, specify:
- central organizing concept;
- included codes;
- inclusion / exclusion boundaries;
- supporting data segments;
- counterexamples or tension cases;
- relation to the RQ and theoretical frame.

Avoid themes that simply mirror interview-question headings unless the analysis genuinely supports that structure.

## 6. Reflexivity

When the researcher is also teacher, practitioner, facilitator, evaluator, community member, or otherwise embedded in the field, reflexivity is methodologically consequential.

Maintain a reflexive record that distinguishes:
- what happened;
- what the researcher noticed;
- immediate interpretation;
- researcher action / intervention;
- possible influence on participants or data;
- assumptions, expectations, or prior commitments;
- later reinterpretation;
- decision made for the next research step.

Reflexivity is not self-criticism for its own sake. Its purpose is to expose how knowledge was produced and how researcher position may have shaped access, interaction, interpretation, or reporting.

## 7. Researcher–teacher dual role

When teaching and researching occur simultaneously:
- separate pedagogical necessity from research data collection where possible;
- record when teaching support may alter participant responses;
- document examples, hints, demonstrations, or corrective feedback that could shape later data;
- avoid interpreting participant compliance with the teacher as independent evidence of genuine agreement;
- preserve participant disagreement, hesitation, resistance, and non-response as analytically meaningful when relevant.

Do not assume that a successful class activity proves the research construct was achieved.

## 8. Triangulation

Triangulation is not simply 'using three data sources.' It should ask whether different evidence streams illuminate the same phenomenon from meaningfully different angles.

Potential forms:
- data-source triangulation;
- method triangulation;
- investigator triangulation;
- theoretical triangulation;
- temporal triangulation.

For each triangulation claim, record:
- focal proposition / finding;
- source A and what it contributes;
- source B and what it contributes;
- source C, if used;
- convergence, complementarity, or contradiction;
- unresolved tension;
- final interpretation and confidence.

Do not force agreement. Divergence is legitimate evidence and may reveal context, role effects, timing effects, or construct ambiguity.

## 9. Negative cases and contradiction

Actively search for:
- participants who do not fit the dominant pattern;
- observations that contradict interview accounts;
- questionnaire responses inconsistent with qualitative narratives;
- researcher expectations not borne out by data;
- temporal changes across sessions.

Negative cases should influence theme boundaries, confidence, or explanation rather than being silently discarded.

## 10. Quality criteria

For interpretivist qualitative research, assess at least:
- credibility;
- transferability;
- dependability;
- confirmability.

Possible strategies include:
- prolonged engagement;
- thick description;
- audit trail;
- peer debriefing;
- member reflection / member checking where epistemologically appropriate;
- negative-case analysis;
- triangulation;
- reflexive journaling.

Do not list these techniques as automatic proof of rigor. Explain how each one actually operated in the study.

## 11. Member checking caution

Participant agreement with the researcher's interpretation is not an objective validity test. Participants may disagree for legitimate reasons, and agreement can be influenced by hierarchy or relationship.

Use member checking / member reflection as one interpretive input, not as a mechanical truth certificate.

## 12. Qualitative claims

Use language proportionate to the design and sample. Avoid unjustified population-wide prevalence claims from purposive qualitative samples.

Prefer formulations such as:
- participants described...;
- a recurring pattern was...;
- within this case / context...;
- the analysis suggests...;
- the data support the interpretation that...

Avoid converting interpretive findings into causal claims unless the design and evidence support that move.

## 13. Reporting traceability

For every major qualitative finding, the reader should be able to trace:

`finding -> theme / category -> representative data -> analytic rationale -> RQ`

When a finding rests primarily on researcher interpretation, say so. When it is strongly corroborated across independent data streams, say that too. Do not collapse the two.
