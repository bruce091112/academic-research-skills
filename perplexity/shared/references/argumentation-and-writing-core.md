# Argumentation and Academic Writing Core — Perplexity Edition

This reference preserves the platform-neutral reasoning and writing logic from the original Academic Research Skills.

## 1. Argument anatomy

For every major research argument, identify:

- **Claim** — what is being asserted?
- **Evidence** — what observations, data, or sources support it?
- **Warrant** — why does that evidence justify the claim?
- **Backing** — what supports the warrant or methodological assumption?
- **Qualifier** — how strongly can the claim be stated?
- **Rebuttal** — what alternative explanation, limitation, or boundary condition could weaken it?

A large quantity of evidence does not rescue a missing warrant. If the logical bridge between evidence and claim is unclear, the argument is weak.

## 2. Alternative explanations

When more than one explanation fits a finding:

1. list the strongest plausible alternatives;
2. compare explanatory scope;
3. compare assumptions and parsimony;
4. compare consistency with observed evidence;
5. compare predictive or explanatory consequences;
6. explain why the preferred interpretation is stronger, if it is.

Do not treat the author's preferred explanation as established merely because competing explanations were not discussed.

## 3. Causal restraint

A causal claim requires more than association. Check at minimum:
- temporal ordering;
- design features that support causal inference;
- plausible mechanism or theoretical warrant;
- consistency with alternative explanations;
- whether the observed data could be explained by confounding, selection, measurement, or reverse causation.

Bradford-Hill-style considerations may guide thinking, but do not turn them into a mechanical causal score. The research design remains decisive.

## 4. Epistemic calibration

Use language proportional to the evidence:

- **Established** — replicated or strongly supported across high-quality evidence;
- **Supported** — evidence is reasonably persuasive but incomplete;
- **Preliminary** — limited, early, single-study, or small-sample evidence;
- **Speculative** — reasoned but not directly evidenced;
- **Contested** — credible evidence points in different directions.

Do not use established-language for preliminary or speculative findings.

## 5. Academic writing principles

### Precision
- use the most specific defensible term;
- define technical terms when first introduced;
- avoid ambiguous pronouns and vague referents;
- name the population, time period, construct, and outcome when they matter.

### Concision
- remove filler and redundancy;
- keep one principal idea per sentence unless ideas are tightly coupled;
- shorten complex sentences before deleting epistemic qualifiers.

### Objectivity
- ground claims in evidence;
- separate observation from interpretation;
- state limitations and plausible alternative interpretations;
- do not write rhetorical certainty unsupported by design or data.

### Register
Adapt to discipline and target audience. Do not impose a universal passive voice or universal third-person style.

For social science and education research, active voice and first-person reflexive statements may be appropriate when they clarify researcher decisions or positionality.

## 6. Paragraph logic

A strong academic paragraph usually contains:

1. a clear topic or argumentative claim;
2. supporting evidence;
3. explanation of how the evidence bears on the claim;
4. a link to the larger argument or next step.

Do not stack citations without explaining their relevance.

## 7. Vague-language control

Replace vague statements with specific evidence when possible:
- `many studies` → identify representative studies;
- `a significant impact` → state the actual effect or clearly say whether `significant` is statistical or practical;
- `in recent years` → give a concrete period when relevant;
- `it is well known` → cite evidence or remove.

## 8. Section-level tense and function

Use tense according to meaning rather than rigid rules:
- literature review: past for completed study findings, present for enduring theories or accepted knowledge;
- methods/results: normally past for what this study did or found;
- discussion: present is often appropriate for interpretation and implications;
- conclusion: present/future for implications and future work.

## 9. Traditional Chinese academic writing

For zh-TW academic prose:
- prefer clear written Chinese over literal English syntax;
- avoid unnecessary passive constructions;
- use shorter sentence units when clauses become deeply nested;
- prefer direct statements over formulaic filler;
- use `本研究` when discipline/institutional style favors it, but do not mechanically eliminate first person when reflexivity or methodological transparency requires it;
- preserve key technical terms consistently across chapters.

## 10. Writing must not outrun evidence

Stylistic improvement never authorizes:
- stronger causal language;
- broader populations;
- removal of limitations;
- changing a null result into a positive result;
- adding unsupported novelty or priority claims;
- converting an author's tentative interpretation into a definitive conclusion.

When clarity and fidelity conflict, preserve fidelity and report the writing problem.
